A Guide to Running TTRPGs

So You Want to Be a
Game Master?

Running a tabletop RPG is one of the most rewarding creative acts you can do with friends. This guide — inspired by Justin Alexander's So You Want to Be a Game Master and the broader TTRPG community — will help you run your first (or fiftieth) session with confidence.

The Core Truth of GMing

Before tactics, systems, and prep — understand the job.

"Your job is not to tell a story. Your job is to create a situation that makes a story possible."
— Justin Alexander, So You Want to Be a Game Master
🎭

You Are a Facilitator

The GM doesn't win or lose. Your success is measured by whether everyone at the table has a great time — including you.

🌍

The World Breathes

NPCs have goals. Factions have agendas. The world moves forward whether or not the players interact with it.

🎲

Dice Tell the Truth

Let the dice fall. Fudging results to protect a narrative robs players of meaningful consequence and agency.

The Six Pillars of Great GMing

Based on Justin Alexander's framework and community best practices.

🗺️

1. Node-Based Design

Design adventures as interconnected nodes — each containing information, characters, or challenges — rather than a linear railroad. Players find their own path between them.

🔍

2. The Three Clue Rule

For every conclusion players must reach, plant at least three clues. One will be missed, one misunderstood — the third gets them there.

🧩

3. Situation, Not Story

Prep situations with stakes and tension, not scripted outcomes. Story emerges from the players' choices, not your outline.

⚡

4. Rulings Over Rules

No rulebook covers everything. Make a fair ruling, keep the game moving, and look it up after the session if needed.

👥

5. Player Agency

Players should feel their choices matter. Avoid predetermined outcomes. "Yes, and" or "Yes, but" almost always beats "No."

📝

6. Prep Smarter

Prep situations, NPCs, and locations — not scripts. Flexible prep adapts to any player choice; rigid scripts crumble at the first unexpected question.

"Skilled GMs don't improvise everything and they don't script everything. They prep the parts that are hard to improvise, and they improvise the parts that are easy."
— Justin Alexander

Session Zero Checklist

Session Zero is a conversation before the campaign begins. Get alignment here and avoid 80% of future table drama.

Game Basics

  • Choose system and edition together
  • Agree on tone: gritty, heroic, comedic, horror
  • Establish setting expectations (homebrew vs. published)
  • Decide on session length and frequency
  • Confirm player count and backup plan for absences

Safety Tools

  • Introduce Lines (hard stops) and Veils (fade to black)
  • Set up an X-Card or similar safety mechanic
  • Discuss real-world topics that are off-limits
  • Agree on how to pause if someone's uncomfortable
  • Normalize checking in mid-session

Character Creation

  • Create characters together, not in isolation
  • Build in connections between PCs from the start
  • Discuss how each character is motivated to adventure
  • Align power levels and mechanical complexity
  • Talk about character death expectations

Table Culture

  • Phones and distractions policy
  • Food and hosting rotation (if in-person)
  • How to handle rules disputes at the table
  • Expectations around spotlight sharing
  • Feedback loop: how will you improve together?
"Session Zero isn't about paperwork. It's about making sure everyone is playing the same game."
— Common TTRPG community wisdom

GM Tools & Techniques

A curated reference of techniques that hold up at the actual table.

Technique When to Use How It Works Category
The Banging Encounter When the session stalls Introduce an immediate complication — someone arrives, an alarm sounds, a wall collapses. Forces action without railroading. Pacing
Yes, And Player proposes something unexpected Accept the premise and build on it. Rewards creative thinking and keeps the fiction moving. Improv
Open Doors Designing any location Every room/location should have at least one obvious exit and one non-obvious one. Prevents players feeling trapped. Design
Fronts (from Dungeon World) Campaign prep Define 2–4 active threats with countdown clocks. The world moves forward on its own timeline, creating urgency without the GM forcing it. Prep
Visible Consequences After any major player choice Show the world reacting. NPCs remember, factions respond, the environment changes. Makes agency feel real. Agency
Scene Framing Starting any scene Open in medias res — in the middle of something already happening. Skip travel, skip waiting. Cut to the interesting moment. Pacing
The Prep Stack Before every session A simple list: 3 NPCs ready to play, 1 location with a twist, 2 potential complications, 1 callback to previous events. Prep

Frequently Asked Questions

Common anxieties, honest answers.

Run a one-shot first. Pick a short published adventure (like D&D's The Lost Mine of Phandelver starter or a Pathfinder beginner box), gather 2–3 friends, and play for 3–4 hours with no commitment. You'll make mistakes — that's the point. Most skills in GMing only develop by doing.
This happens every session without exception. The answer is to prep situations, not scripts. If you know what each NPC wants and what each location contains, you can improvise freely. When truly stuck, stall for 30 seconds ("give me a moment") and ask yourself: what would make this scene more interesting?
The community is divided, but the strongest argument is: don't fudge. Consequence is what makes victory meaningful. If death is impossible, survival means nothing. That said — you can absolutely design encounters that are survivable, telegraph danger, and give players information to make smart choices. That's better than secretly overriding the dice.
Enough to run the game, not enough to recite it. Justin Alexander's principle of "rulings over rules" applies here. The core mechanic of most systems fits in a sentence. Learn that, learn the most common combat and skill actions, and look everything else up as it comes up. Most players respect a GM who says "I'll rule X for now and we'll look it up after."
Talk to them privately first, not in front of the group. Most disruptive behavior comes from boredom, unmet expectations, or not realizing the impact. A direct, non-accusatory conversation ("Hey, I noticed X — is everything okay?") resolves 90% of cases. If behavior continues after a clear conversation, protecting the rest of the table is the priority.
Give them one goal, one quirk, and one secret. That's it. You don't need a backstory novel — you need enough to improvise consistently. Three attributes you can play make an NPC more memorable than ten pages you'll never reference.
  • Goal: What do they want right now?
  • Quirk: How do they behave/speak unusually?
  • Secret: What are they hiding?
The most common answer is D&D 5e — because it has the largest support network, most published adventures, and most players who already know it. But the best system is the one your players are excited to play. Pathfinder 2e rewards tactical depth. Ironsworn is excellent for solo or duo play. Blades in the Dark excels at heist fiction. Match the system to the experience you want to create.
